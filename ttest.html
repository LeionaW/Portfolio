Does Your Writing Hand Beat Your Other Hand?

What We Did

In class, each student wrote the sentence "I am amazing" as many times as possible in one minute using their writing hand and then again using their other hand. We collected data from nine students. We also asked each student a yes/no question and compared the writing-hand scores between the two groups. Finally, we compared the class writing-hand average to Dr. Jacques's benchmark score.

Test 1: Class Writing Hand vs. Dr. Jacques Benchmark

The class writing-hand mean was lower than Dr. Jacques's benchmark score. The difference was 1.667 repetitions, with the class averaging 6.333 repetitions and the benchmark equal to 8 repetitions. The p-value was 0.0011, which provides strong evidence that the class average was different from and lower than the benchmark.

Test 2: Yes Group vs. No Group

Students who answered "yes" had a slightly higher writing-hand average than students who answered "no." The difference between the two groups was only 0.150 repetitions. The p-value was 0.8398, which indicates very little evidence of a meaningful difference between the two groups.

Test 3: Writing Hand vs. Other Hand

Students wrote more repetitions with their writing hand than with their other hand. The average difference was 3.778 repetitions, with means of 6.333 and 2.556, respectively. The p-value was 0.00000647, providing very strong evidence that students performed better with their writing hand.

In Plain Words

Most students were able to write the sentence more times with their writing hand than with their other hand. The class average was also lower than Dr. Jacques's benchmark score. However, answering "yes" or "no" to the class question did not appear to be related to how many repetitions students completed.

What I Would Do Differently

If I repeated this study, I would collect data from a larger group of students because a sample of nine people is very small. I would also randomize which hand is used first since students may become tired or improve with practice after the first trial. In addition, I would allow multiple trials for each hand and average the results to reduce the effect of random variation. Finally, I would collect more background information from participants so we could examine other factors that might affect writing speed, such as age, handedness, or previous writing experience.
